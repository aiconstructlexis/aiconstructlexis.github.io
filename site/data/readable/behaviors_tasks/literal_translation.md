# Literal translation
**Type:** Behavioral indicator  
**Supporting papers:** 4  
**Also known as:** Literal translation of idioms  

## State of the Field

**Space of definitions.** Across papers, the prevailing usage treats literal translation as a word-for-word, phrase-by-phrase, or compositionally faithful rendering that preserves source structure but misses figurative meaning or a natural, idiomatic target-language expression, most often for idioms and proverbs. Most authors frame this as an observable error pattern, for example describing models as "often rendering them literally as compositional expressions," though one dataset paper also uses literal proverb translations as a resource for understanding the original wording and cultural nuances. A smaller line of work extends the same label beyond idioms to domain-specific text.

**Variants.** Papers mostly study this behavior in idiom and proverb translation, and one paper explicitly names it "literal translation of idioms." Some work applies the same label in domain-specific tax and financial education translation, where literalness is described as using descriptive phrases such as "income that is subject to tax" instead of "taxable income," and a finance example contrasts a natural rendering like "a leap of faith" with outputs such as "a leap into the void" or "a leap into the unknown." It is also related to speech translation: one paper reports that speech-to-text systems on idiomatic data often revert to literal translations.

**Space of elicitation and scores.** No measurement instrument is linked to it.

**What it is taken as evidence of.** No construct is linked to it, and the papers do not explicitly present it as evidence of a broader construct.
