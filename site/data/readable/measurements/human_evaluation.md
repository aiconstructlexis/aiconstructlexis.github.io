## State of the Field

**What it is.** Across papers, the term covers several manual assessment protocols rather than one fixed format. The prevailing usage is a human or expert judgment procedure for rating model outputs, often through pairwise preference comparisons, Likert or numeric scoring, or rubric-based review, though some papers narrow it to spot-checking or verification of automated labels, and others use humans to perform the task itself as a baseline. Papers also refer to nearby variants such as human annotation, verification, review, manual evaluation, and user study, and the studies range from small sampled checks to large annotation exercises with over 10,000 judgments.

**What it is claimed to measure.** Papers use human evaluation to measure 49 constructs and 20 behavioral indicators, so its reported scope is broad rather than centered on one agreed target. The most frequent reported targets are helpfulness, conciseness, factuality, fluency, and harmlessness. Behavioral-indicator claims are similarly dispersed across many tasks rather than concentrated on a single behavior.

**Space of uses.** Among the 12 linked instruments, LLM-as-a-judge is the prevailing companion: papers report that human evaluation correlates with it, and many definitions use human judgments as the reference for validating GPT-4 or other automatic evaluators, or for deciding cases when evaluators disagree. Smaller lines of work relate human evaluation to AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard, E-GSM, and FaithEval, plus 7 other instruments. Across definitions, papers position it both as a headline assessment for open-ended outputs and as an auxiliary validation layer through spot-checking, manual verification, or human baselines for datasets and tasks.

**Concerns raised.** Several papers describe human evaluation as the gold standard, but also note practical and reliability limits. Some explicitly call it costly, cumbersome, or time-consuming to scale, while one paper on human grading says the process can be noisy and that graders may need training to agree on a rubric. Other papers pair it with spot-checking or verification protocols, and some report only moderate inter-annotator agreement.
