# BoolQ
**Type:** Measurement instrument  
**Supporting papers:** 207  
**Also known as:** Boolq, BOOLQ  

## State of the Field

**What it is.** Across papers, BoolQ is most commonly described as a dataset or benchmark of yes/no question answering grounded in a passage, with each example consisting of a question, a passage, and a binary answer. The prevailing descriptions frame it as reading comprehension or binary QA, and many also present it as testing commonsense reasoning or factual reasoning. Some authors further emphasize that the questions are naturalistic, naturally occurring, or unprompted, and one paper specifies 15,942 examples split into 9,427 train, 3,270 validation, and 3,245 test; smaller departures describe it as a closed-book QA dataset or use it as a text classification task.

**What it is claimed to measure.** Papers use BoolQ to measure 9 constructs and 8 behavioral indicators. At the construct level, the prevailing framing is commonsense reasoning, with recurring use for natural language understanding, reasoning, short-context ability, and benchmark overfitting, plus 4 other constructs. At the behavioral level, it is most often reported as measuring machine reading comprehension, question answering, zero-shot question answering, multiple-choice question answering, and downstream task performance, plus 3 other indicators, so the claims are spread across several targets even though reading comprehension, question answering, and commonsense reasoning recur most often.

**Space of uses.** In use, papers most often place BoolQ inside multi-benchmark evaluation suites and report it as a zero-shot accuracy task, though some also use 32-shot or few-shot settings. It is linked to 4 other instruments, most often SuperGLUE, lm-evaluation-harness, a commonsense reasoning benchmark, and OLMES. Beyond headline evaluation, a smaller line of work uses it as source or warm-up data, a held-out test for generalization, or an auxiliary check for calibration and self-correction.

**Concerns raised.** Explicit concern-focused discussion is sparse, but one paper uses BoolQ to measure benchmark overfitting by evaluating the impact of data contamination. Contamination is therefore the clearest stated concern attached to BoolQ in these papers.
