# HarmBench
**Type:** Measurement instrument  
**Supporting papers:** 69  
**Also known as:** Harmbench, HARMBENCH  

## State of the Field

**What it is.** Across the surveyed papers, HarmBench is most commonly described as a benchmark or dataset of harmful instructions, behaviors, prompts, or questions used to evaluate jailbreak success and safety behavior under adversarial attacks. Definitions also call it a standardized framework or evaluation framework, and some papers emphasize built-in judging machinery, referring to an official classifier of response harmfulness or a default judge. A smaller line of work treats it as an out-of-distribution test set distinct from AdvBench, or uses subsets such as JBB-Behaviors slices to test generalization and avoid leakage.

**What it is claimed to measure.** Papers link HarmBench to 5 behavioral indicators and 5 constructs. The prevailing usage is to measure jailbreaking, often through attack success rate or harmful-response judgments; papers also use it to measure harmful request refusal, adversarial robustness, safety, and attack transferability. Overall, the behavioral claims center on jailbreak-related evaluation, while construct-level claims are spread across several safety-related targets rather than a single construct.

**Space of uses.** No explicit instrument-to-instrument relationship is reported, but papers often use HarmBench alongside AdvBench, and some also pair it with JailbreakBench, XSTest, HEx-PHI, and DirectHarm4. In these settings it is positioned as a standard jailbreaking or safety benchmark, sometimes as a fixed harmful-question evaluation set and sometimes as a transfer or generalization check. Several papers rely on its scoring machinery, using the provided harm classifier, the default judge, or the benchmark's response-harmfulness classifier to score attack success or response harmfulness.

**Concerns raised.** Several papers explicitly manage data leakage and separation from training or attack-construction data. One paper stresses that its HarmBench experiment was "totally not involved in the training process," another uses a JBB-Behaviors subset "to prevent the data leakage," and another treats HarmBench as an out-of-distribution set distinct from AdvBench. One study also excludes copyright-related behaviors, stating that they should be evaluated differently.
