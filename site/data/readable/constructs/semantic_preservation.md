# Semantic preservation
**Type:** Construct  
**Supporting papers:** 26  
**Also known as:** Meaning preservation, Meaning accuracy, Semantic fidelity  

## State of the Field

**Space of definitions.** Across papers, the prevailing usage treats semantic preservation as the degree to which a transformed text retains the original meaning, semantic content, or core information, whether the transformation is stylization, paraphrasing, simplification, revision, adversarial rewriting, or watermark removal. Several definitions phrase this as avoiding content drift or preserving the same underlying message, for example by "ensuring a minimal impact on the original semantics" or "maintaining the meaning expressed in the original text." A smaller line of work shifts the target of preservation: one paper defines it for a selected subset that retains the semantic information of an original benchmark question set, and one paper, under the name meaning accuracy, adds preservation of intended meaning and logical consistency with the expected answer. One paper also frames it as a latent ability of a model to modify text while retaining meaning, while most others describe a property of the text or transformation itself.

**Space of facets.** Papers use several nearby labels for largely the same idea, most often meaning preservation and semantic fidelity, with one paper using meaning accuracy. Across these labels, the shared core is retention of original meaning or core information, but the same family of terms is applied to different scopes: most often source-to-rewrite fidelity, and in a smaller usage, semantic coverage of a benchmark subset. Some papers place it alongside other evaluation dimensions such as fluency, conciseness, layness, and simplicity, and one revision paper explicitly treats it as something to balance against text simplification. A simplification paper further notes that meaning preservation is often lacking correlation with simplicity, so some authors keep the two separate rather than collapsing them.

**Space of measurement.** Papers link semantic preservation to 2 measurement instruments and 1 behavioral indicator. Among the linked instruments, Human evaluation is used most often, followed by LLM-as-a-judge, and the only linked behavioral indicator is Paraphrasing: judges rate outputs for meaning preservation on a 1-5 Likert scale, and one LLM evaluator scores simplified text for meaning preservation, fluency, and simplicity. Other papers operationalize the construct through a reference-based meaning preservation metric, a reading-comprehension task, semantic similarity between original and perturbed outputs, and Wasserstein distance between embeddings of a selected question subset and the full benchmark. These operationalizations mostly follow the dominant definition of retained meaning under rewriting or transformation, though the Wasserstein-distance version applies the construct to semantic coverage of a dataset subset rather than to a rewritten text.

**Links.** Outside measurement, the recorded links are all related links. One paper reports that semantic preservation is related to text generation quality, stating that higher semantic similarity between attacked and original text reflects better readability and semantic preservation. Another paper relates paraphrasing to semantic preservation by describing paraphrase pre-training as enabling rewriting "without changing the semantics." No paper reports a causal relationship or a correlation involving this construct.
